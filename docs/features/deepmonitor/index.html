A hardware monitor that tells you what the numbers mean

DeepMonitor shows every sensor of your CPU, graphics card, memory and drives live, with minimum and maximum. Then it does what HWMonitor and HWiNFO leave to you: it picks the temperature to trust, marks the sensors that lie, and learns what is normal for this PC under each kind of load.

What it shows

One window, one tree, the way people know it from HWMonitor: every reading with its value, its minimum and its maximum since you opened it.

CPU

  • Package and every core temperature
  • Load of the whole CPU and of every core
  • Current and base clock
  • Core voltage (Vcore)

Graphics card

  • Core temperature
  • Load and memory (VRAM) use
  • Fan speed

Memory and drives

  • RAM load, used and available
  • Page file use
  • Used and free space on every drive

Motherboard

  • Board and chipset temperatures
  • Voltage rails (12 V, 5 V, 3.3 V)
  • Fan speeds

Read through LibreHardwareMonitor running in the background, as Windows does not hand these to programs directly.

Pause freezes the table for a screenshot, Reset clears the minimum and maximum before a test, and Save Data writes the whole table to a TXT or CSV file. Colours follow the value: green, amber from 70 °C, red from 83 °C.

Which CPU temperature is the real one

HWiNFO can list eight or more CPU temperatures, and "which one is real" is one of the most common questions on its forum and on Tom's Hardware. The short answer:

  • Intel: CPU Package. The CPU's own short average of its hottest sensor.
  • AMD Ryzen: Tctl/Tdie. On current Ryzen chips the two are the same number. Only first-generation Ryzen used an offset between them, as the author of HWiNFO explains on its forum.
  • Single cores jump from one moment to the next. Useful for spotting one hot core, not for judging the cooler.

DeepMonitor does this choice for you. It reads the sensor's id, not its name, takes CPU Package on Intel and Tctl/Tdie on AMD, and that one number is the CPU temperature everywhere in PC Workman: in the alerts, in the history and in hck_GPT's answers. The cores stay in the table for whoever wants them.

Sensors that lie: 127 °C on the motherboard, 0 RPM on the card

Many motherboards report inputs that nothing is connected to. On Nuvoton and ITE chips they often show up as AUXTIN or TMPIN and read a fixed 127 °C, or jump between 0 and 125. Another monitor puts that in red at the top of the list, and people start pricing a new board. A real 110 °C on a motherboard would be felt and smelled.

DeepMonitor treats a reading as not real when it sits on a value no board reaches, never moves, or jumps between extremes. It marks the sensor, explains why, and leaves it out of the alerts and of the learned norm.

0 RPM on the graphics card is usually not a broken fan. Most modern cards stop their fans under light load on purpose and start them at around 50 to 60 °C. DeepMonitor says so instead of raising an alarm, and warns only when the card is hot under load and the fan still does not spin.

Your normal, per workload

Is 82 °C a lot? In a game, on most laptops, no. With nothing running, yes. A fixed threshold cannot tell the two apart, so DeepMonitor does not use one. In the background it saves the readings with the load at that moment, and PC Workman learns a normal range for five kinds of load: idle, light, medium, heavy and gaming.

On my PC it has been learning for 137 days: idle 39 to 56 °C, light 45 to 61 °C, gaming 59 to 72 °C for the graphics card. An alert compares a reading with the norm for the same kind of load, so a hot game is fine and a hot idle desktop is not. Loads this PC rarely sees are learned along the way and shown apart, so they do not hold back the rest.

Voltages get the same treatment with statistical process control: PC Workman watches the 12 V, 5 V and 3.3 V rails and Vcore for slow drift, the kind a fixed limit only notices when it is too late.

History you can go back to

HWMonitor and HWiNFO show the session in front of you; for anything longer you start a log file and open it in another program. PC Workman keeps the history itself: minute data for 7 days, hourly for 90 days, and a daily summary for good.

From the learning card, CPU history, RAM history and GPU history open Activity Memory: pick yesterday at 21:00 and see the temperature, the load and the process that caused it, or put this month next to the same month half a year ago, before a Windows update.

Ask hck_GPT "is 82 °C a lot" and it answers from these numbers: your norm, the load right now, and the highest reading of the day.

Where the others are better. HWiNFO reads far more sensors, has alerts with actions per sensor, feeds the RTSS overlay and has plugins; if you tune voltages by hand, keep it. AIDA64 has SensorPanel for a second screen, benchmarks and long reports. HWMonitor is tiny and opens in a second, and HWMonitor PRO adds graphs and remote monitoring (CPUID). DeepMonitor is for the question behind the numbers: is this PC fine, and what is normal for it?

Questions

Which CPU temperature should I look at?

On Intel, CPU Package. On AMD Ryzen, Tctl/Tdie, which on current chips is the same number. Single cores jump too much to judge the cooler by. DeepMonitor picks this one temperature by the sensor's id.

Why does my motherboard show 110 or 127 °C?

Almost always an input with nothing connected, often AUXTIN or TMPIN. It reads a fixed value, often 127 °C, or jumps between 0 and 125. DeepMonitor marks it as not real and leaves it out of alerts; if in doubt, compare with the temperatures in the BIOS.

Is 82 °C too hot for a CPU?

Under load, usually not: many desktop and most laptop CPUs slow down to protect themselves only around 95 to 100 °C. With nothing running, it points to a cooling problem. DeepMonitor compares the reading with this PC's norm for the same load.

Why is my graphics card fan at 0 RPM?

Most modern cards stop their fans under light load on purpose and start them at around 50 to 60 °C. It is a problem only if the card is hot under load and the fan still does not spin.

Is DeepMonitor a replacement for HWiNFO?

For most people, yes. Enthusiasts who tune voltages by hand may keep HWiNFO next to it for its sensor count, per-sensor alerts and plugins.

Does DeepMonitor need LibreHardwareMonitor?

For the CPU temperature, voltages and fans, yes, because Windows does not hand them to programs directly. Load, clocks, memory, drives and NVIDIA graphics cards work without it.
