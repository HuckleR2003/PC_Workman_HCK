Who it is for

  • School computer labs. Twenty to forty PCs, one IT teacher, no time to walk from desk to desk with a thermometer.
  • Small offices. Ten to fifty PCs and nobody whose job is to notice that one of them is about to run out of disk.
  • Internet cafés and gaming rooms. Machines under load all day, where a clogged cooler shows up as a customer complaint.
  • Computer repair shops. A quick, honest health picture of every machine that comes in.

What the admin sees

A tile for every PC with its state colour, its name and three numbers: CPU temperature, memory, and the system drive. Click a tile and the full PC Workman of that machine opens, read only: My PC, its history, its processes.

Warnings in plain words, written by hck_GPT for the admin, for example:

PC-14: drive C: has 4% free, Windows updates will not fit.
PC-07: CPU 15 °C hotter than its own normal under the same load. Check for dust.
PC-22: three restarts without a shutdown in a week.

Each PC is judged against its own learned normal, the same way DeepMonitor does it on a single PC, so a lab of different machines does not drown in false alarms from one fixed threshold.

  • Comparisons: lab averages, the five worst PCs, the one that runs hotter than its identical twins, the age of drivers across the lab.
  • History of the whole lab for more than six months, with the busy hours (lessons, shifts) visible.
  • A report for the head or the owner: the state of the hardware, what to replace first, which PCs go to service.

What it never collects

Classroom tools mostly watch people: screens, websites, what a pupil types. That is where the trouble starts. In Poland the data protection authority fined a school 52,511 PLN for cameras in classrooms without a legal basis and ordered them off within 14 days (Prawo.pl). Central watches hardware, not people.

  • School mode: programs are shown as groups (browsers, games, office) instead of a full list, so the data cannot tell what one pupil was doing.
  • A visible agent: every PC says it is part of a lab managed by PC Workman Central. There is no hidden mode.
  • A pack for the head and the data protection officer: the list of collected data, an information notice template for pupils, parents and staff, a template of the data protection impact assessment (GDPR art. 35), and a default retention period that can be shortened.

How it works

  1. The agent is PC Workman running without a window on every PC. It collects locally all the time, learns that machine's normal and keeps its history, even when Central is off.
  2. Central runs on the admin's PC and only reads from the agents.
  3. Pairing with a code, like Bluetooth: a code on the agent, typed into Central. The connection is encrypted with a key from the pairing, so nobody else on the network can read another PC.
  4. Local network only in version 1. No cloud, no accounts. The data does not leave the building.
  5. Version 1 is view only. No remote killing of processes, restarts or installs: a remote action on forty PCs is the biggest risk a lab can take. Remote actions may come later, behind permissions and with a log.
  6. Quiet install for admins: an installer with parameters (silent install, pairing code), so forty PCs are not a day of clicking.

Why it is worth building

In a computer lab you learn about a broken PC during the lesson: a student raises a hand because the machine hangs, and its drive has been full for a week. An office is the same, only the hand goes up on Monday at eight. Every one of those computers knew about the problem earlier. Nobody asked it.

  • A gap in the market. Classroom tools watch people (screens, websites, locks). Remote management tools watch patches and scripts, live in the cloud and are written for IT departments. None of them says in plain words which computer in the room is about to fail, and why.
  • The foundation already works. On one PC, PC Workman collects in the background, learns the normal for every workload, keeps the history and explains readings through hck_GPT. Central is the same work on every seat, plus what one PC cannot do: compare itself with its twins.
  • Twins are an edge a single PC does not have. A lab usually has twenty identical computers. If one of them runs 12 °C hotter than the rest during the same lesson, that is not a hot day, it is dust or dried paste. That signal is close to certain and needs no fixed threshold.
  • Privacy as an argument, not an obstacle. Schools fear monitoring because monitoring usually watches people. Central watches only hardware from day one, so a head teacher can deploy it without fearing the next data protection decision.

Stability first

An agent on forty computers that freezes the system once a week means forty tickets a week and the end of a school's trust. That is why stability is the foundation of Central, not a separate topic. Before the first lab screen exists, PC Workman goes through an audit:

  • no query to the system (graphics card, WMI, sensors) runs on the interface thread, and every one has a time limit;
  • nothing that stops or ends processes runs in the version 1 agent;
  • the agent has a budget: the target is under 1% of the CPU and a few dozen MB of memory, measured on the weakest PC in the room, not the strongest;
  • an error in the agent ends with a log entry and an agent restart, never with a window in front of a student.

The plan, step by step

Every step ends with something that works and can be shown.

  1. PC Workman stability. The freeze audit, time limits, a console only when something is wrong, and support for many resolutions. The same things every PC in a lab needs.
  2. A windowless agent. PC Workman running as a quiet agent: collects, learns, keeps history, exposes reading only. Pairing by code and encryption.
  3. The room view. Tiles with a status colour, a click opens that PC's PC Workman read-only, hck_GPT warnings for the admin.
  4. Comparisons and history. Room averages, the worst five, the PC that stands out from its twins, more than half a year of history with lesson hours.
  5. Report and pilot. A report for the head, the GDPR pack, the first school lab and the first office. Pricing only after the pilot.
  6. Later, behind permissions. Remote actions with a log (who, what, when, with undo), many rooms and buildings, export to inventory systems.

Technical rules, written down up front

Things that are easy to skip at the start and cost the most later:

  • The agent connects to Central. Student PCs listen on no port. One firewall rule on the admin's PC instead of forty, and nothing to attack on the seats.
  • The agent works without Central. When the admin's PC is off or the network is down, the agent keeps collecting and fills in the history when it is back. A gap in the chart must never read as a failure.
  • A room plan, not a list of names. Tiles laid out like the seats in the room (row, seat), not as DESKTOP-7F3K2. A teacher looks for "third in the second row", not a Windows name.
  • History that fits. One-minute samples for 7 days, then hourly averages. Estimate for 40 PCs and half a year: around 100-150 MB, which any admin PC can hold.
  • One clock. Lab PCs have drifting clocks. Central corrects the agents' timestamps, or a comparison "during the same lesson" lies.
  • Agent versions. Central reads agents at least one version older, and updates roll out in stages: one PC first, then the rest of the room.
  • Deployment the way schools do it. A signed installer with parameters (silent install, pairing code), to push through Group Policy or Intune. Schools block unsigned programs, and they are right to.
  • Roles. A teacher sees their room, the head sees the report, the admin pairs computers and sets the retention period. In version 1 every role only reads.
  • An access log. Central records who looked at which PC and when. With hardware data that is a formality, but the data protection officer will ask for it first.
  • Quiet at the seat. The agent shows no balloons or windows to the student and does nothing heavy during a lesson. The student only sees a note that the PC belongs to a lab with Central.
  • Inventory on the side. Model, CPU, memory, drive and age of every seat in one table. Schools do this today in a spreadsheet, by hand, once a year.
  • Energy. An idea to test in the pilot: how many hours the PCs run outside lessons, and what that costs in electricity. A head understands that argument at once, because it is about the budget.

Risks we keep in mind

  • Privacy. One screenshot of a student in the data and the product has no right to exist in a school. The "never collects" list is a principle, not a setting.
  • Remote actions. Restarting forty PCs in the middle of a test is damage no UNDO can reverse. That is why version 1 only looks.
  • Scope. Central is built by one person after hours. Version 1 has to be small and solid: the room view, comparisons, the report. The rest waits for the pilot.
  • Free competition. Veyon is free and open. Central does not compete with it at running a lesson; it does what Veyon does not do at all, and can run next to it.

Questions

Does it watch what pupils or employees do?

No. Hardware state only: temperatures, load, drives, drivers, and programs as names with average use. Never screens, keystrokes, websites, file contents or names, or Windows user names. In school mode programs are shown as groups.

Is it compatible with GDPR in a school?

It is built for it: hardware data only, on the school's own network, a visible agent, and a pack for the head and the data protection officer. The school stays the controller and decides on the legal basis.

Does it need the internet or a cloud account?

No. Local network only, pairing with a code, encrypted connection. The data does not leave the building.

Can it restart or fix PCs remotely?

Not in version 1, on purpose. It shows and explains. Remote actions may come later, behind permissions and with a log.

How much will it cost?

Not set yet. The plan: free for a few PCs, a yearly licence per lab above that, an education licence for public schools, well below business remote management tools. Pilot places are free.

When will it be available?

It is in preparation. The first step is a pilot in one school lab or one small office.
