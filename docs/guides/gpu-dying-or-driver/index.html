Is your graphics card dying, or is it the driver? The symptoms, in order

A graphics card costs too much to replace on a hunch. Most "dying GPU" stories end with a driver, a setting or heat. A few do not, and they have a clear signature.

By Marcin Firmuga·Published 2026-11-27·8 min read
Short answer

One question settles most cases: does the problem appear before Windows loads the graphics driver? Coloured dots, lines or a checkerboard on the BIOS screen or the boot logo point at the hardware, because no driver is running there. If the problem only shows up in Windows or in games, start with the driver, settings, heat and power, and suspect the card only when it survives all of them and follows the card into another PC.

The one test that settles it

Restart and watch the screen before Windows starts: the motherboard logo, the BIOS setup screen if you open it. Windows' graphics driver is not loaded yet, so the card draws that picture on its own. If you see artifacts there, the driver cannot be the cause. Before blaming the card, rule out the cable and the monitor: another cable, another port on the card, another monitor or TV if you have one.

The second decisive test is the same idea from the other side: move the card to another PC, or put another card (or your processor's integrated graphics, if it has them) into yours. A fault that follows the card is the card. A fault that stays with the PC is the PC.

The order to rule things out

  1. Defaults. Reset any overclock, undervolt and custom fan curve. Unstable tuning produces exactly the symptoms people read as a dying card.
  2. A clean driver. Remove the driver completely (the vendor's clean install option, AMD Cleanup Utility, or DDU in Safe Mode), install the current version, and if the problem began after an update, try the previous one.
  3. Heat. Watch the GPU temperature and hotspot in the game that fails. Artifacts or crashes that only appear after twenty minutes of load point at cooling, which is fixable: dust, fan, thermal paste or pads.
  4. Power. Use a separate cable from the power supply to each PCIe connector, check that every connector is fully seated, and consider whether the power supply is close to its limit.
  5. Video memory. If artifacts persist at defaults and normal temperatures, a dedicated VRAM test (OCCT has one) shows whether the card's memory returns errors.
  6. Another PC. The last word, as above.

Things that sound bad and usually are not

  • Coil whine: a buzz from components at high frame rates. Annoying, not failing. A frame rate limit often quiets it.
  • Fans at 0 RPM on the desktop: most modern cards stop their fans at light load on purpose.
  • One driver reset after an update: common, and usually nothing to chase.
  • High temperature in a heavy game: normal within the card's range. What matters is a temperature above this card's own normal for the same game.

When it is the card

If the artifacts appear before Windows, or the fault follows the card into another PC at default settings with a clean driver, you have the evidence a seller asks for. Write down the dates, the symptoms and the tests you ran, take a photo of artifacts on the BIOS screen, and contact the seller or the card maker about warranty before you open the card yourself.

Short answers

How do I know if my graphics card is dying?

Artifacts before Windows loads its driver (BIOS screen, boot logo), or a fault that follows the card into another PC at defaults with a clean driver.

Can a driver cause artifacts?

Yes, inside Windows and games. On the BIOS screen no driver is loaded, so artifacts there point at the hardware.

Are black screens in games a sign of a dying GPU?

Not by themselves. A black screen that comes back is usually a driver reset caused by tuning, the driver, heat or power.

Is coil whine a sign of a failing GPU?

No. It is a buzz from components under load, common at very high frame rates.
